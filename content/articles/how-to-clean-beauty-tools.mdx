Beauty tools spend their lives in contact with skin oil, product residue and a humid bathroom — and most of them get cleaned far less often than makeup brushes. The good news is that nearly every tool on a vanity can be kept in good shape with mild soap, lukewarm water and, above all, proper drying. Electric devices are the exception: they need their own manual.

## Why tool hygiene matters

Anything that touches your face can carry oil, dead skin and microbes back to it the next time. That matters most for tools used near the eyes or on broken skin. FDA advises that any [instrument placed near the eye should be clean](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/eye-cosmetic-safety), notes that applicators left on a countertop can pick up bacteria, and recommends not sharing eye products. Its general guidance on [using cosmetics safely](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-consumers-cosmetics/using-cosmetics-safely) adds washing your hands before use and not sharing makeup.

Sponges show what happens when tools are neglected. A 2019 Aston University study, [reported by Safety+Health](https://safetyandhealthmagazine.com/articles/19261-researchers-find-superbugs-in-most-used-makeup-including-on-sponges), found that used blending sponges had especially high levels of bacteria, and that most of the sponges tested had never been cleaned. Contamination doesn't mean you'll get sick, but it's a good argument for washing and drying tools properly.

## The basic method (for most non-electric tools)

1. **Remove residue.** Wipe off product, oil and makeup with a tissue or clean cloth.
2. **Wash.** Use a drop of mild, fragrance-free soap or a gentle cleanser and lukewarm water. Avoid very hot water, which can damage some materials.
3. **Rinse well** until no soap remains.
4. **Dry completely.** Pat dry, then let the tool air-dry on a clean towel in open air before storing.
5. **Disinfect if appropriate.** On hard, non-porous tools that tolerate it, a wipe with rubbing alcohol after washing is common. The CDC notes alcohol works best in [60–90% solutions and doesn't penetrate protein-rich material well](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/disinfection-sterilization/chemical-disinfectants.html), which is why cleaning comes first. Check the manufacturer's care instructions, since alcohol can dull or damage some plastics, coatings and stones.

### Facial rollers and gua sha

Because these tools glide over bare skin, often with oil or serum, they collect a film quickly. Wipe them after each session and give them a proper wash with mild soap and lukewarm water. Avoid long soaks and abrasive cleaners, which can scratch some stones, and keep water away from the metal frame and roller joints. Dry them completely before returning them to a pouch or case. If you're still deciding between the two, our [facial rollers vs. gua sha](/facial-rollers-vs-gua-sha) guide compares them.

### Silicone cleansing brushes and other devices

Manual silicone brushes can usually be washed like any other tool. Powered brushes, microcurrent tools, LED devices and anything with a battery or charging port should be cleaned exactly as their manual says. Manufacturers' instructions vary: Panasonic, for example, tells users of one silicone brush to [wash the appliance after each use](https://help.na.panasonic.com/answers/how-to-use-the-silicone-brush-eh-xc10), while other devices are only splash-resistant and shouldn't be rinsed under running water. If you've lost the manual, search the brand's website using the model number. Unplug before cleaning, and don't use alcohol or harsh cleaners on device surfaces unless the manual allows them.

### Eyelash curlers and tweezers

These are small, hard tools used close to the eyes and on freshly plucked follicles, so they're worth cleaning often. Wipe the curler pad and frame to remove mascara and oil, wash the metal with a little soap and water when needed, and replace the pad when it cracks or develops a deep groove. For tweezers, wash off any residue, then wipe the tips with rubbing alcohol if the finish tolerates it, and let them dry. Don't share either tool. Facial razors need the same care plus regular blade replacement; our guide to [dermaplaning at home](/dermaplaning-at-home) covers how often to swap them.

### Sponges

Damp sponges are the trickiest because they stay wet inside. Wash them with soap and water after every use, squeezing until the water runs clear, then let them air-dry in open air rather than in a closed case. Replace sponges that tear, stay stained or smell — washing won't fix a worn-out sponge. Brushes have their own routine; see [how to clean makeup brushes](/how-to-clean-makeup-brushes).

## Drying and storage

Drying is where a lot of tool hygiene goes wrong. A tool put away damp, or kept in a closed case in a steamy bathroom, stays wet long enough for microbes and mold to thrive.

## When to replace instead of clean

Some tools reach the end of their life. Replace sponges regularly, curler pads when they crack, tweezers that no longer meet at the tips, rollers with loose or squeaky frames and any stone that chips. If you develop an eye infection, FDA advises discarding the eye cosmetics you were using; clean or replace the applicators and tools that went with them too, and see a doctor about the infection.

## The bottom line

Most beauty tools need nothing more than mild soap, lukewarm water and complete drying, done often. Clean anything that touches your eyes or bare skin after each use or every few uses, wash sponges every time, follow the manual for anything electric, and never share eye tools. If your skin reacts or you develop an eye infection, see a board-certified dermatologist or eye doctor.
