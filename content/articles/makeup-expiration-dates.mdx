Most of the makeup in a typical drawer has no printed expiration date, which makes it easy to keep a foundation for years or a mascara well past its best. Makeup does go off, though — and for eye products, the risk is more than a patchy finish. Here's what FDA and dermatologists say about shelf life, how to read the labels you do get, and the signs that a product belongs in the bin.

## Why most makeup has no expiration date

FDA explains that [no U.S. law or regulation requires cosmetics to carry an expiration date](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-labeling/shelf-life-and-expiration-dating-cosmetics). Manufacturers are responsible for making sure their products are safe, which includes working out how long they hold up, but they don't have to print that on the package.

FDA also describes any shelf-life guidance as a rule of thumb. A product stored somewhere hot or in direct sunlight can break down well before a typical date, while one kept cool, dry and closed may last longer. That's why your own eyes and nose matter as much as any timeline.

Sunscreen is the exception on most vanities: it does carry a date. The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) notes that FDA requires sunscreens to keep their original strength for at least three years, and that you should throw one out once its date has passed.

## How to read the open-jar symbol

Some products carry a Period After Opening (PAO) symbol: a small drawing of an open jar with a number and the letter M beside it. As the [AAD explains](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/replace-makeup-sunscreen), the number is how many months the product is meant to last after you first open it — so "12M" means about a year from opening.

The symbol isn't required in the U.S., so plenty of products won't have one. When there's no symbol, write the date you open a product on the packaging with a permanent marker, or on a small label on the base.

## General timelines by product type

Without a date on the package, dermatologists suggest general guidelines. Liquid, cream and eye products tend to spoil soonest; dry powders last longest.

These ranges draw on guidance from FDA, the AAD and [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/when-should-you-toss-your-makeup), and sources don't all agree on exact numbers. Treat them as a starting point, and always follow a PAO symbol or manufacturer guidance where there is one.

## Mascara deserves extra caution

Mascara has the shortest lifespan for a reason. FDA notes that eye-area cosmetics tend to have shorter shelf lives because applicators — especially mascara wands — pick up bacteria and fungi each time you use them, and that this contamination may lead to eye infections. The AAD cites research that found *Staphylococcus aureus* bacteria in mascara after three months of use.

If your mascara dries out, throw it away. FDA advises [not adding water or saliva](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-labeling/shelf-life-and-expiration-dating-cosmetics) to bring it back, since that introduces more bacteria.

## Eye infections: what to do with your makeup

FDA's [eye cosmetic safety guidance](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/eye-cosmetic-safety) is clear: if you have an eye infection or the skin around your eye is inflamed, stop using eye cosmetics, and throw away any you were using when the infection started. Start fresh once it has cleared. For eye symptoms such as pain, discharge or changes in vision, see a doctor or eye care professional promptly.

## Storage habits that help makeup last

How you store and handle makeup affects how long it stays usable. FDA's tips for [using cosmetics safely](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-consumers-cosmetics/using-cosmetics-safely) and the AAD's advice line up:

- Keep products in a clean, dry place at room temperature, out of direct sunlight — not in a hot car.
- Close lids and caps tightly after each use.
- Wash your hands before applying makeup.
- Clean brushes and sponges regularly; see our guide to [cleaning makeup brushes](/how-to-clean-makeup-brushes).
- Keep a small, edited collection you'll actually finish. Our guide to [organizing a skincare routine](/how-to-organize-a-skincare-routine) has ideas for a vanity layout that makes older products easy to spot.

If you decant products for travel, label each container with the date — our guide to [packing skincare for a carry-on](/how-to-pack-skincare-for-a-carry-on) covers hygienic decanting.

## Sharing and store testers

FDA warns against sharing eye cosmetics, because another person's germs can be harmful to you, and notes that store testers used by many people are even more likely to be contaminated than products at home. If you sample makeup in a store, use a fresh, single-use applicator such as a clean cotton swab, and avoid testing eye products directly on your eyes.

## The bottom line

Makeup does expire, even when there's no date on it. Replace mascara every few months, expect liquid and cream products to last less time than powders, follow any open-jar symbol, and write opening dates on everything else. Toss anything that changes in smell, color or texture, discard eye products after an eye infection, and keep your collection stored cool, closed and clean.
