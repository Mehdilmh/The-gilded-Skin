Body mists have become a popular way to wear scent: large bottles, gentle prices and an easy, spray-all-over feel. But they aren't simply cheaper perfume. They're made to do a slightly different job, and knowing the difference helps you choose the one that fits how you actually wear fragrance.

## The short answer

A body mist is a light fragrance for casual, generous use. A perfume — usually sold as an eau de toilette (EDT) or eau de parfum (EDP) — is more concentrated and meant to be applied sparingly. Neither is "better"; they suit different moments and budgets.

## What's in a body mist

Ingredient lists vary, but many body mists follow a similar pattern. Take eos's Cashmere Body Mist in Vanilla Cashmere as an example: retailer listings give its ingredients as denatured alcohol, water and fragrance first, followed by glycerin, sodium hyaluronate (a form of hyaluronic acid) and aloe leaf juice.

Two things follow from that. First, alcohol and water make up most of the formula, which is part of why mists feel light and fade faster. Second, humectants such as glycerin and sodium hyaluronate can make a mist feel pleasant on skin, but in a spray they're not a substitute for a moisturizer. If your skin is dry, moisturize first and treat the mist as fragrance.

## What you get from a perfume

A perfume gives you a more defined fragrance from a smaller amount. Classic eaux de toilette show what that looks like: Elizabeth Taylor's White Diamonds, launched in 1991, is listed by The Perfume Shop with lily, neroli and tuberose on top, orris, narcissus and amber in the heart, and sandalwood, patchouli and oakmoss in the base — a fuller, layered composition than a typical single-theme body mist.

Concentration labels aren't standardized, so an EDT from one brand can be lighter or stronger than another's. Our guide to [choosing a perfume online](/how-to-choose-a-perfume-online) explains what the labels do and don't tell you.

## Sensitive skin

A body mist isn't automatically gentler than perfume. Both contain fragrance, and FDA notes that fragrance ingredients may be [listed simply as "fragrance"](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics) on U.S. labels, even though some components can cause reactions. If your skin is sensitive, [test a new product on a small area](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products) first, and consider spraying fragrance on clothing instead of skin.

## The bottom line

Choose a body mist for a light, low-cost scent you're happy to reapply, and a perfume when you want a more defined fragrance from a few sprays. Many people use both: a mist for everyday, a perfume for when it matters.
