Microcurrent devices are the gadgets that promise a "workout for your face": two rounded metal probes glided over the cheeks, jaw and brow while a faint current runs between them. They're popular, often expensive, and surrounded by confident before-and-after marketing. This guide explains what they are meant to do, how little independent evidence there is, what an FDA clearance claim tells you, and the practical details worth comparing if you still want one.

## What a microcurrent device does

Microcurrent devices deliver a very low-level electrical current to the skin with the aim of stimulating the facial muscles underneath. Manufacturers usually describe the result as temporary toning, lifting or contouring, and most recommend short, frequent sessions — often several times a week — to maintain any effect. That's different from the other device categories on this site: [LED masks](/led-face-masks-what-to-compare) use light, and [ultrasonic skin devices](/at-home-ultrasonic-skin-devices) use high-frequency vibration.

Almost every handheld microcurrent device also needs a conductive gel or primer on the skin. FOREO, for example, explains that [the gel is what carries current from the device into the skin](https://www.foreo.com/mysa/best-conductive-gel-for-microcurrent-mg), and that using a device dry tends to be uncomfortable and less effective. It's worth noting that this explanation comes from a company that also sells gel.

## What the evidence shows (and doesn't)

This is the part marketing tends to skip. When we searched PubMed and dermatology resources for this guide, we didn't find large, independent, controlled trials of the low-level handheld microcurrent devices sold for home use. Much of the published material comes from manufacturers, brand-funded studies, patents or small studies of in-clinic treatments that combine microcurrent with other technologies, such as radiofrequency. Those results can't simply be applied to a battery-powered device used at home.

That doesn't mean microcurrent does nothing — it means the evidence isn't strong enough to say what you can reasonably expect. A fair way to frame it:

## What "FDA-cleared" actually means

Many microcurrent devices advertise FDA clearance. Clearance comes through FDA's [510(k) premarket notification process](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k), in which a company shows its device is substantially equivalent to a device that is already legally marketed — the "predicate." FDA then issues a letter clearing the device for sale.

Two things follow from that:

- **Cleared isn't approved.** FDA's own training materials say it's [inappropriate to describe a 510(k)-cleared device as "FDA approved"](https://www.fda.gov/training-and-continuing-education/cdrh-learn/510k-program-transcript); approval is a separate, more demanding pathway.
- **Clearance is about equivalence, not results.** It doesn't mean FDA has confirmed that a device will visibly lift or tone your face.

If a brand claims clearance, you can check it yourself in FDA's public [510(k) database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm) by searching the company or device name. Look at the device name and intended use on the record, not just the word "cleared."

## Who shouldn't use one

Because these devices pass current through the body, manufacturers publish contraindications, and they're worth reading before you buy rather than after. NuFACE's [safety guide](https://www.mynuface.com/blogs/media/can-you-use-a-microcurrent-device-if-you-have-fillers-botox-or-implants-a-safety-guide) says microcurrent isn't for people with a cardiac pacemaker or other implanted electronic device, and also lists seizure disorders, cancer in the treatment area, clotting disorders and broken or infected skin; it advises caution during pregnancy, noting there's no safety data either way. FOREO's BEAR 2 manual warns against use with [any implanted medical device or other electronic instrument](https://www.foreo.com/manuals/bear-2) and notes that electronic monitoring equipment such as ECG monitors may not work properly while it's running.

Lists differ between brands, so read the manual for the exact model. If you have a heart condition, an implant of any kind, or you're pregnant, ask your doctor before using one. If you have a skin condition, a board-certified dermatologist can tell you whether a device makes sense for you.

## The bottom line

At-home microcurrent devices aim to tone facial muscles with a low-level current, but independent evidence for the handheld versions is limited, and any effect is generally described as temporary. Treat "FDA-cleared" as a claim to verify, not a promise of results; read the contraindications first; and count the cost of conductive gel and the time each session takes. If you're unsure whether a device is safe for you, talk to your doctor or a board-certified dermatologist before buying.
