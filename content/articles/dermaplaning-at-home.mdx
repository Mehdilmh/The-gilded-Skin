Dermaplaning has moved from the treatment room to the bathroom counter. The at-home version is simpler than it sounds: a small, single-blade tool glides over the face to remove fine "peach fuzz" and a little surface buildup. Done carefully on the right skin, it's a low-key grooming step. Done with a dull blade, over breakouts or on irritated skin, it's an easy way to end up with nicks and redness.

## What dermaplaning is (and what the home version isn't)

In a clinic, dermaplaning is performed by a dermatologist or trained professional using a surgical blade to remove fine hair and a layer of dead skin cells. Cleveland Clinic describes it as [generally considered safe](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22680-dermaplaning), while noting possible risks such as infection, scarring and changes in skin color — and that the hair removal is temporary because the hair grows back.

At home, you're using a consumer facial razor or "dermaplaning tool," which is essentially careful face shaving. That distinction matters: the goal is a light pass that removes hair and leaves skin feeling smooth, not a deep exfoliation. Pressing harder or going over the same patch repeatedly doesn't make it work better; it just makes irritation more likely.

## Will the hair grow back thicker?

No. This is the most common worry, and it isn't supported. A Cleveland Clinic dermatologist explains that cutting hair at the surface [doesn't change how it grows](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/dermaplaning-at-home/). Regrowth can feel coarser at first because a cut hair has a blunt tip instead of a naturally tapered one, and that feeling softens as it grows. Like a haircut, shaving doesn't make hair grow faster, thicker or darker.

If you notice new, coarse or dark facial hair appearing that wasn't there before — rather than regrowth of what you shaved — that's worth mentioning to a doctor, since it can have causes that have nothing to do with shaving.

## The real risks

The realistic downsides of at-home face shaving are small but common:

- **Nicks and cuts**, especially around the jaw, nose and upper lip. The AAD notes that a [dull blade makes cuts more likely](https://aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/hair/how-to-shave) and also causes irritation.
- **Irritation and redness**, from too much pressure, too many passes, dry skin or a blade that's past its best.
- **Breakouts**, particularly if you shave over existing pimples. The AAD advises people with acne to shave lightly and never try to shave off acne, because both can make it worse.
- **Ingrown hairs**, less common with fine facial hair but possible. Our guide to [preventing razor bumps](/how-to-prevent-razor-bumps) covers the habits that help.

Retinoids deserve a specific note. The AAD advises stopping retinol and prescription retinoids [two to five days before facial waxing](https://aad.org/skin-care-basics/how-to-wax), and not waxing at all within six months of isotretinoin, because these products can leave skin more fragile. Shaving isn't the same as waxing, but the same sensitivity is a good reason to ask your prescriber before taking a blade to retinoid-treated skin.

If you have rosacea, the National Rosacea Society suggests [gentle cleansers, lukewarm water and avoiding anything that stings or burns](https://www.rosacea.org/patients/skin-care/face-shaving-tips-for-skin-with-rosacea), and checking with your dermatologist about what's right for your skin.

A few details make a difference. Shaving in the direction of hair growth is the AAD's general advice for reducing irritation. Light pressure matters more than angle: if you can feel the blade scraping, you're pressing too hard. Leave active ingredients such as exfoliating acids or retinoids out of your routine that evening so freshly shaved skin isn't hit with something strong. A simple barrier-supporting moisturizer is enough — our guide to [ceramides](/what-are-ceramides) explains what to look for.

Morning sunscreen is always a good idea, and especially after any kind of exfoliation. If you'll be outside for long, our guide to [reapplying sunscreen over makeup](/how-to-reapply-sunscreen-over-makeup) covers top-ups during the day.

How often? There's no evidence-based schedule. Many people find every few weeks is plenty, which is roughly how long fine hair takes to become noticeable again. If your skin is red, stinging or breaking out, give it longer.

## Tool hygiene and when to replace the blade

A blade that touches your face should be treated like any razor. The AAD recommends [replacing blades or disposable razors after five to seven shaves](https://aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/hair/how-to-shave), rinsing during use and storing razors somewhere dry so they can dry completely between uses. For small facial razors, that usually means:

- Rinse off hair and product after each session, then let the blade air-dry fully before capping or storing it.
- Store it out of the shower, in a dry drawer or case.
- Replace it sooner if it tugs, feels dull, shows rust or has been dropped.
- Never share it.

## The bottom line

At-home dermaplaning is gentle face shaving, and it won't make your hair grow back thicker. Keep it simple: clean, dry skin, a sharp blade, light strokes in the direction of hair growth, and a plain moisturizer afterward. Skip it over breakouts, rosacea or eczema flares and retinoid-sensitized skin, and see a board-certified dermatologist if you have a skin condition or notice unexpected new hair growth.
