The most useful morning skincare routine is the one you'll do every day. That usually means a short one. Instead of starting from a list of products, start from the job each step does — and only add a product when it has a job to do.

## The four-step order

The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) notes that the order in which you apply skincare can affect how well products work. Its [recommended sequence](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/care/apply-skin-care-certain-order) is straightforward:

1. **Cleanse** with a gentle cleanser, then pat dry.
2. **Treat**, if you use a treatment or medicated product — apply it right after cleansing.
3. **Moisturize and/or apply sunscreen.**
4. **Makeup**, if you wear it.

That's the whole framework. Everything else is detail.

## Step 1: A gentle cleanse

The AAD's [face-washing guidance](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/care/face-washing-101) is refreshingly simple: wet your face with lukewarm water, apply a gentle, non-abrasive cleanser with your fingertips, rinse, and pat dry with a soft towel. It specifically advises against scrubbing, and notes that washcloths, mesh sponges or anything other than fingertips can irritate skin.

The AAD suggests washing twice a day — once in the morning and once at night — and after heavy sweating.

## Step 2: Treatment (only if you have one)

This is where a serum or a product your dermatologist recommended goes. If you don't currently use a treatment, skip this step — a routine without it is still complete.

The AAD cautions that using too many products may irritate your skin, particularly when combining more than one anti-aging product. If you already use active ingredients at night, a morning treatment isn't automatically necessary.

## Step 3: Moisturizer

Moisturizer helps when your skin feels dry or tight. The AAD's face-washing guidance suggests applying moisturizer if skin is dry or itchy, and to be gentle around the eyes. Some people find a separate moisturizer unnecessary in the morning when their sunscreen already feels comfortable; let your skin, not a checklist, decide.

## Step 4: Sunscreen

If there's one non-negotiable morning step, this is it. The AAD recommends that everyone use a [broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with an SPF of 30 or higher](https://www.aad.org/media/stats/prevention-and-care/sunscreen-faqs) on skin not covered by clothing, and that it be reapplied about every two hours outdoors, or after swimming or sweating, according to the directions on the label.

Because sunscreens are regulated as over-the-counter drugs in the U.S., they carry expiration dates — check yours.

## Adding new products safely

When you introduce something new, change one thing at a time and give it a fair trial. If several products change at once and your skin reacts, you won't know which one caused it.

The AAD also recommends [testing a new product before applying it to your face](https://www.aad.org/news/how-to-test-skin-care-products-before-use) — particularly if you have sensitive skin — and talking with a board-certified dermatologist if you can't identify what's irritating your skin.

## Make the routine easy to repeat

Consistency comes from convenience. Keep your morning products together, in order, within reach of the sink — our guide to [organizing skincare on a vanity](/how-to-organize-a-skincare-routine) shows how. A routine that's visible and short is one you're far more likely to keep.

## The bottom line

Cleanse gently, add a treatment only if you have one, moisturize if your skin needs it, and finish with broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher. Add products slowly and deliberately. For persistent concerns — acne, sensitivity, pigmentation — a board-certified dermatologist can tailor a routine to your skin in a way a general guide can't.
