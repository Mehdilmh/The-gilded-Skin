At-home facial devices range from simple tools to sophisticated electronics with prices to match. The best way to avoid an expensive drawer-dweller is to answer a few questions honestly before you buy. This checklist works for any device type; for specifics, see our guides to [LED face masks](/led-face-masks-what-to-compare), [cleansing brushes](/facial-cleansing-brushes-what-to-compare) and [ultrasonic skin devices](/at-home-ultrasonic-skin-devices).

## 1. What, specifically, do you want it to do?

"Better skin" isn't a goal a device can be judged against. Name one specific outcome — a cleansing ritual you enjoy, a calming massage, a particular concern a dermatologist has discussed with you — and check that the device's intended use matches it. If you have an active skin concern, a dermatologist visit first can save you from buying the wrong thing.

## 2. What does independent evidence say about this type of device?

Look for research on the device category from sources other than the brand. Even well-studied categories have gaps: a [systematic review of LED trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29356026/), for example, noted small sample sizes and widely varying treatment parameters. Brand-funded studies aren't worthless, but ask who ran them, how many people took part, whether there was a comparison group and what was actually measured.

## 3. Can you verify the regulatory claim?

If a device claims to be FDA cleared, it has generally gone through FDA's [510(k) process](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k), which establishes substantial equivalence to an existing legally marketed device. It's not the same as approval, and it doesn't verify every advertising claim. You can search FDA's [510(k) database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm) by company or device name. Note that many simple tools, such as manual rollers, don't make claims that would require clearance.

## 4. Is it safe for you?

Read the device's warnings and contraindications — many brands publish the manual online — before buying, not after. Pay attention to any listed conditions, medications or circumstances in which the device shouldn't be used, and ask your doctor or pharmacist if anything applies to you. Also consider friction: the American Academy of Dermatology notes that [tools other than fingertips can irritate skin](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/care/face-washing-101) during cleansing.

## 5. Will you actually use it?

Most devices depend on consistent use. Check the recommended session length and frequency, and be realistic about whether it fits your mornings or evenings. A ten-minute session several times a week, for weeks, is a real commitment.

## 7. How do you clean and store it?

Devices that touch skin need cleaning after use. Check the cleaning instructions, whether parts are removable and whether it's water resistant. Plan where it will live — our guide to [organizing a vanity](/how-to-organize-a-skincare-routine) covers storage zones.

## 8. What happens if it isn't for you?

A generous return window is valuable for devices, because you won't know whether you'll use it until you try the routine. Check whether opened devices can be returned, who pays return shipping and how long you have.

## The bottom line

A facial device is worth considering when it serves a specific goal, has credible support for its type, is safe for you and fits your routine and budget over time. If it fails any of those questions, a simpler option — such as a [facial roller or gua sha](/facial-rollers-vs-gua-sha) — or simply a consistent routine may be the better investment.
