Ultrasonic facial devices — many of them from Korean brands — have become a popular step in at-home routines. They're easy to use and feel pleasant, but the marketing can blur the line between a home gadget and a clinic treatment.

## How ultrasonic devices work

You apply a gel or serum, then glide the device's metal head slowly over your skin. It vibrates at ultrasonic frequencies — too fast to hear — producing gentle, high-frequency vibration on the surface. Sessions typically last several minutes per area.

In medicine, ultrasound has been studied for helping substances move into the skin, but that research uses clinical equipment and specific drug molecules. Whether a home device meaningfully changes how a cosmetic serum works is not well established.

## Reading the claims

Some brands quote precise figures, such as large percentage increases in absorption or elasticity. Unless you can see an independent study — including who funded it, how many people took part and whether there was a comparison group — treat those numbers as marketing. Devices that make medical claims should have an FDA record you can check in the [510(k) database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm); clearance itself is explained on FDA's [510(k) page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k).

## The bottom line

An at-home ultrasonic device can be an enjoyable part of a skincare ritual, especially if you like massage-style tools. Just don't confuse it with a clinical treatment, be skeptical of precise percentage claims, and factor in the ongoing cost of gels. If you want simpler and cheaper, a [facial roller or gua sha](/facial-rollers-vs-gua-sha) gives you the massage without the electronics.
