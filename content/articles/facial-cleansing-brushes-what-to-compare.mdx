Facial cleansing brushes promise a "deeper clean" than your hands. Before deciding whether one belongs in your routine, it helps to know what dermatologists recommend for ordinary cleansing — because it's simpler than the marketing suggests.

## Start with the baseline: your fingertips

The American Academy of Dermatology's [face-washing guidance](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/care/face-washing-101) recommends wetting your face with lukewarm water, applying a gentle, non-abrasive cleanser with your fingertips, rinsing and patting dry. It specifically notes that using a washcloth, mesh sponge or anything other than your fingertips can irritate your skin, and advises resisting the urge to scrub.

That doesn't mean a brush is harmful for everyone. It does mean a brush is optional, and the benefit has to be weighed against added friction.

## Using a brush gently

If you decide to try one, start slowly. Use light pressure — let the brush move, rather than pressing it into your skin — and follow the manufacturer's timing. Start with infrequent use rather than twice daily. Clean and dry the head after each use, and don't share it.

Watch for signs that it's too much: redness that lingers, stinging, tightness, flaking or new sensitivity. If they appear, cut back or stop. As with any new product, the AAD's advice to [test new things cautiously](https://www.aad.org/news/how-to-test-skin-care-products-before-use) and see a board-certified dermatologist for unexplained irritation applies.

## The bottom line

A cleansing brush is a comfort purchase, not a skincare necessity. Your fingertips and a gentle cleanser already meet dermatologists' basic recommendation. If you want a brush, soft silicone, light pressure and gradual use are the safest starting points.
