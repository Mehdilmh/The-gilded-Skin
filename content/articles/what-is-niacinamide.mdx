Niacinamide shows up on so many labels — serums, moisturizers, sunscreens, even cleansers — that it's easy to assume it does everything. It's a genuinely well-studied ingredient with a good tolerability record, but the evidence is more modest than some marketing suggests. Here's what it is, what research suggests it may help with, and how to use it sensibly.

## What niacinamide is

Niacinamide, also called nicotinamide, is a form of vitamin B3. In skincare it's applied topically, usually in a water-based serum or a moisturizer. It isn't the same thing as niacin (nicotinic acid) taken by mouth, which is a different form of B3 with its own dosing and side effects — so advice about oral niacin doesn't apply to your serum, and vice versa.

According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/niacinamide), niacinamide is generally considered gentle and safe for the skin, which is part of why it's added to so many products, including some sunscreens.

## What the research suggests

Niacinamide has been studied for several cosmetic and dermatological uses. A [2024 review in *Drug Delivery and Translational Research*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38722460/) summarizes clinical evidence for cosmetic uses as well as some skin conditions. A [2021 review on aging and pigmentation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34439563/) found that clinical trials report improvements in signs of aging and hyperpigmentation, but also noted there's no convincing evidence yet of exactly how niacinamide does this at a molecular level.

One frequently cited trial is a [2005 split-face study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16029679/) in which 50 women applied a 5% niacinamide moisturizer to half of the face for 12 weeks. Researchers reported improvements in fine lines, hyperpigmented spots, red blotchiness and elasticity on the treated side. It's useful evidence, but it's one relatively small study of one formula.

If you have acne, rosacea, melasma or another skin condition, niacinamide can be part of a routine, but it isn't a stand-in for care from a board-certified dermatologist. For other ingredients often used for redness and uneven tone, see our guide to [azelaic acid](/what-is-azelaic-acid).

## Concentrations you'll see

Cleveland Clinic notes that most skincare products contain 5% niacinamide or less, though amounts vary. You'll also see serums marketed at 10% or higher. Higher numbers make for eye-catching labels, but a higher percentage isn't automatically more effective — and for some people it's more likely to cause irritation.

A sensible approach:

- If your skin is sensitive, start with a product at the lower end of the range, or one where niacinamide is part of a moisturizer rather than a concentrated serum.
- Use one niacinamide product at a time; it's easy to double up without noticing, since it appears in so many formulas.
- Give any new product several weeks before judging it.

When you compare products, look at where niacinamide sits in the ingredient list and whether the brand states a percentage, since not every brand does. A short, fragrance-free formula is easier to judge and less likely to irritate, and a moisturizer or sunscreen that already contains niacinamide may be all some routines need. Packaging matters less than with fussier ingredients, but a pump or dropper bottle is easier to keep clean than an open jar.

## Irritation and who should be careful

Niacinamide is generally well tolerated, but Cleveland Clinic lists possible effects including itching, mild burning, redness and, rarely, allergic reactions. Irritation is more likely when you're introducing several new products at once, using a high-strength serum, or your skin barrier is already compromised.

Stop using the product if you notice persistent stinging, redness or itching. If a reaction is severe or doesn't settle once you stop, see a board-certified dermatologist.

## Pairing niacinamide with other actives

Niacinamide is one of the easier ingredients to combine. Cleveland Clinic notes that it's often used alongside retinol, where it may help soothe the irritation retinoids can cause, and lists vitamin C among ingredients that pair well with it. Combinations still vary by formula, so follow each product's directions and introduce them one at a time.

## Patch test first

Even gentle ingredients can disagree with individual skin. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends [testing a new product](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products) on a small area, such as the inner arm, twice a day for 7 to 10 days before using it on your face. Our step-by-step [patch-testing guide](/how-to-patch-test-skincare) explains how to do it and what to look for.

## The bottom line

Niacinamide is a form of vitamin B3 with a solid tolerability record and research suggesting it may help with tone, redness, oiliness and barrier comfort, though many studies are small. Choose a sensible concentration — most products contain 5% or less — introduce it on its own, patch test first and pair it with sunscreen. For a diagnosed skin condition, let a board-certified dermatologist guide your routine.
