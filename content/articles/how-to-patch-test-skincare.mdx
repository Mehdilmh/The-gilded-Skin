A new serum or moisturizer can be exciting right up until your face turns red and itchy. A patch test won't catch every possible problem, but it's a simple way to find out how your skin responds to a product before you put it on your face. Here's the method the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) recommends, how to read the results, and when a dermatologist should take over.

## Why patch testing is worth the wait

Skin can react to products in two main ways. Mayo Clinic explains that [irritant contact dermatitis](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/contact-dermatitis/symptoms-causes/syc-20352742) — the more common type — happens when something damages the skin's outer protective layer, while allergic contact dermatitis is an immune reaction to a specific substance, such as a fragrance or another ingredient in a personal care product. Allergic reactions can take hours or even days to appear, which is why a single dab on your wrist isn't enough.

Patch testing is especially worth doing if you have sensitive skin, eczema or rosacea, if you've reacted to products before, or if you're trying an active ingredient for the first time — for example a retinoid, an acid or even a gentle option like [niacinamide](/what-is-niacinamide).

## How to patch test a product at home

The AAD's [method for testing skincare products](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products) is a repeat application on a small area over about a week:

A few practical tips make the results easier to trust:

- **Test one product at a time.** If you test three at once and react, you won't know which one caused it.
- **Keep a note** of the product, start date and anything you notice. A quick photo on your phone helps if you're unsure whether the area looks different.
- **Don't test on irritated or broken skin**, or right after shaving the area.
- **Remember that the face is often more sensitive** than the arm, so start slowly once you move to your face, especially with active ingredients.

The same approach works for things that sit on the skin during a treatment, such as a conductive gel used with a [microcurrent facial device](/microcurrent-facial-devices-explained).

The AAD notes that some ingredients, such as retinol and glycolic acid, can cause irritation that's normal and temporary. The line between "expected" and "too much" isn't always obvious, so if a reaction is uncomfortable, spreading or lasting, stop and ask a professional rather than pushing through.

A reaction also isn't always to a new product. Older products can change as they age; our guide to [makeup expiration dates](/makeup-expiration-dates) explains the signs a product has gone off.

## When to see a dermatologist

An at-home test tells you whether one product suits you. It can't tell you which ingredient is the problem if you keep reacting to different products. The AAD recommends seeing a board-certified dermatologist if it's hard to pinpoint what's causing your skin to react.

Mayo Clinic also suggests seeing a healthcare provider if a rash is so uncomfortable that it disrupts sleep or daily life, is severe or widespread, or doesn't improve within about three weeks. Signs of infection, such as fever or pus, need prompt medical attention.

## Professional patch testing

For suspected allergic contact dermatitis, dermatologists use a medical test also called patch testing. According to the AAD's guide to [patch testing](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/eczema/types/contact-dermatitis/patch-testing-rash), small amounts of common allergens are applied to the skin under patches, and the dermatologist checks for reactions over several days. Cleveland Clinic notes that the [process takes about a week](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/patch-test) and that patches are usually placed on the back.

During testing you'll usually be asked to keep the patches dry and avoid scratching, since that can affect the results. Once the cause is identified, the dermatologist can help you plan how to avoid it — which often means learning to read ingredient lists for specific names. The AAD's [contact dermatitis overview](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/eczema/types/contact-dermatitis) has more on diagnosis and care.

<Callout title="Passing isn't the final word" tone="note">
Passing a home patch test is reassuring, but it doesn't rule out a reaction later, especially on more sensitive areas like the face or around the eyes. Introduce new products one at a time, and if your skin barrier feels fragile, a [barrier-friendly routine](/cold-weather-skin-barrier-routine) may help keep things simple while you test.
</Callout>

## The bottom line

To patch test skincare, apply your normal amount to a quarter-sized spot on the inner arm or elbow twice a day for 7 to 10 days, one product at a time. Wash it off and stop at the first sign of redness, itching or swelling, and get emergency care for facial swelling or trouble breathing. If you keep reacting and can't tell why, a board-certified dermatologist can use professional patch testing to find the cause.
