Sunscreen in the morning is the easy part. Reapplying at lunchtime, over foundation and concealer, is where most routines quietly fall apart — nobody wants to wash off their makeup and start again. The good news is you don't have to. The less good news is that some of the most convenient options give less protection than their labels suggest. Here's how to choose.

## When you actually need to reapply

Both the AAD and FDA give the same rule. When you're outdoors, [reapply sunscreen every two hours, and immediately after swimming or sweating](https://aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/how-to-apply-sunscreen). FDA advises reapplying [at least every two hours, and more often if you're sweating or swimming](https://fda.gov/drugs/understanding-over-counter-medicines/sunscreen-how-help-protect-your-skin-sun). A higher SPF doesn't buy you a longer gap.

In practice, that means reapplying before a lunchtime walk, an afternoon school run, an outdoor event or a long drive with sun on one side of your face — not necessarily at your desk. Reapplication matters: a 2024 study of facial sunscreen use found that knowing how to apply sunscreen wasn't enough by itself, and that [skipping reapplication undermined protection](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11745782/).

## Why makeup with SPF isn't enough

It's tempting to count your foundation's SPF as reapplication. Cleveland Clinic explains why that doesn't work: SPF is tested using [a much thicker layer of product than anyone wears as makeup](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/is-the-sunscreen-in-your-makeup-enough), so a normal layer of foundation or powder gives far less than the number on the label. Wear a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher sunscreen under your makeup, as in our [day makeup routine](/day-makeup-look), and treat SPF makeup as a bonus.

## Step one: blot before you reapply

Before adding anything, press a blotting paper or clean tissue over oily areas, especially the forehead, nose and chin. Removing excess oil and sweat helps the next layer sit evenly rather than sliding or pilling, and stops you dragging makeup around. Pat — don't rub.

### Sunscreen sticks

A stick is the closest thing to reapplying your morning sunscreen without disturbing everything. The AAD suggests [using a stick or lotion on the face](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/how-to-use-stick-spray-sunscreens) rather than a spray. Glide it lightly over each area several times — one quick swipe leaves very little product — then press it in with clean fingertips or a damp sponge. Clear or tinted sticks blend more easily over makeup than white ones.

### Lotions pressed on with a sponge

If you're outside for hours, a lotion or fluid sunscreen gives you the most control over the amount. Dot a generous layer over the face and press it in with a damp makeup sponge. It's messier than a stick, but it's the most dependable way to get close to a full layer.

### Sprays: carefully, and not at your face

Sprays are convenient for the body, but the AAD is clear that you should [never spray sunscreen around or near your face or mouth](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/how-to-use-stick-spray-sunscreens). Instead, spray it into your hands and then pat it onto your face. The AAD also advises rubbing spray sunscreen in after spraying, avoiding sprays on windy days when they're easier to inhale and keeping aerosol sunscreen away from flames. That makes sprays most useful for your arms, chest, neck and scalp rather than your makeup.

### Powder sunscreens: a top-up, with caveats

Powder sunscreens are the most makeup-friendly option, and they double as shine control. The catch is quantity. SPF is measured with a thick layer of product, and it's hard to apply that much powder evenly without it looking heavy. That's the same reason Cleveland Clinic gives for makeup SPF falling short. If you use a powder, press on several layers rather than one light dusting, and lean on a stick or lotion when you'll be in strong sun for long. Check that it's labeled broad spectrum with an SPF of 30 or higher.

## How much is enough?

The AAD and FDA say most adults need [about one ounce — roughly a shot glass — to cover the whole body](https://aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/how-to-apply-sunscreen). The face, ears and neck need a generous share of that, which is more than most people use. FDA lists [commonly missed spots](https://fda.gov/drugs/understanding-over-counter-medicines/sunscreen-how-help-protect-your-skin-sun) including the ears, nose, lips, back of the neck and hairline — all easy to forget when you're focused on not smudging your makeup.

## The bottom line

Wear a broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen under makeup, and when you're outdoors, reapply at least every two hours and after swimming or sweating. Blot first, then press on a sunscreen stick or lotion for the most reliable top-up. Keep sprays away from your face, and treat powders and SPF mists as convenient extras rather than your main protection. If you notice a new or changing spot on your skin, see a board-certified dermatologist.
