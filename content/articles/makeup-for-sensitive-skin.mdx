If foundation leaves you itchy by lunchtime or mascara makes your eyes water, you're not imagining it. Sensitive skin can react to ingredients other people never notice. The good news is that most people with reactive skin can still wear makeup; it mostly comes down to choosing simpler formulas, testing before you commit and being gentle at the end of the day.

## Start with fewer products and fewer ingredients

The fewer ingredients a product contains, the fewer chances it has to set off a reaction. Advice from the American Academy of Dermatology, summarized by [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/general-health/health-tip-sensitive-skin-523214.html), is to favor products with short ingredient lists.

The same logic applies to your whole routine. A pared-back lineup of five or six products is easier on skin and much easier to troubleshoot: if something does flare, you have fewer suspects. Our [day makeup look](/day-makeup-look) is a good template for a short, light routine.

## Fragrance-free, not "unscented"

Fragrance is one of the most common reasons people react to cosmetics. The AAD notes that if you're allergic to fragrance, you may develop [contact dermatitis](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/eczema/types/contact-dermatitis/symptoms) where you apply a personal care product.

Labels can be confusing. The AAD explains that ["unscented" and "fragrance free" mean different things](https://www.aad.org/news/how-to-test-skin-care-products-before-use): unscented products may contain a fragrance added to mask other smells, while fragrance-free products have none added. Be cautious, too, with botanical products, which can contain fragrance-related ingredients.

## Powder and mineral options

Dermatologists often point to powder formulas as a gentler starting point. In an [AAD news release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/top-10-cosmetic-dos-donts-for-women-with-sensitive-skin), dermatologist Dr. Draelos noted that powder cosmetics contain very few preservatives and minimal ingredients that can irritate. Mineral powders are one common example. Powder isn't automatically safe for everyone, though; dry skin may find it emphasizes flakes, and any product can cause a reaction.

For bases, a sheer, simple formula may be easier to tolerate than a long-wear, full-coverage one. Our comparison of [skin tint vs. foundation](/skin-tint-vs-foundation) can help you decide how much coverage you really need.

HealthDay's AAD-based tips also suggest avoiding waterproof makeup when you can, because removing it usually requires stronger solvents.

## Patch test before you commit

## Remove makeup gently

Taking makeup off can irritate skin as much as wearing it. Use a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser or remover, press and hold rather than scrubbing, and pat dry. Keep removal simple; layering toners and strong actives afterward can add to the problem. If you use a toner, our guide to [witch hazel vs. hypochlorous acid](/witch-hazel-vs-hypochlorous-acid) explains why some formulas sting more than others. It's also wise to skip extra heat on reactive days; we cover why in [is a facial steamer worth it](/facial-steamer-worth-it).

## Eye makeup for sensitive eyes

Eyes and eyelids are especially reactive. The AAD notes that eyelids are a common site for contact dermatitis because their skin is so thin. FDA's [eye cosmetic safety guidance](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/eye-cosmetic-safety) adds practical habits:

- Wash your hands before applying eye products.
- Keep applicators and anything placed near your eyes clean.
- Don't share or swap eye cosmetics, and be wary of store testers.

A simple eye look of mascara and perhaps one matte shadow usually means fewer triggers than glitter, layered liners and lash glue. If an eye product irritates you, stop using it, and see a doctor if the irritation persists. If you curl your lashes, our [heated eyelash curler guide](/heated-eyelash-curler-guide) covers hygiene and heat safety.

## When to see a dermatologist

Occasional mild stinging may just mean a product isn't right for you. But see a board-certified dermatologist if you notice signs the AAD associates with [contact dermatitis](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/eczema/types/contact-dermatitis/symptoms), such as intense itching, a swollen or warm rash, burning, hives or blisters, especially if they keep coming back. A dermatologist can perform formal patch testing to identify the specific ingredient involved, which makes it far easier to choose products you can wear comfortably.

## The bottom line

Makeup for sensitive skin is less about finding a magic product and more about reducing the odds of a reaction: fewer products, short ingredient lists, fragrance-free labels, powder where it suits you and gentle removal. Patch test new products for a week or so before they go on your face, keep eye makeup simple and clean, and let a dermatologist investigate rashes that keep returning.
