Azelaic acid has quietly become one of the most searched skincare ingredients of 2026. Unlike many trends, it has real research behind it — and some clear limits.

## What azelaic acid is

Azelaic acid is an acid that occurs naturally in grains such as barley and wheat; skincare products use a lab-made version. It's used in both prescription treatments and over-the-counter serums and creams. It's generally described as having anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects and as helping with uneven pigmentation.

## What the research supports

A 2023 [systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1111/jocd.15923) of randomized trials found that topical azelaic acid performed better than placebo for **acne**, **rosacea** and **melasma** (a common form of hyperpigmentation). The authors said more trials are needed before drawing conclusions about **skin aging**.

For rosacea specifically, a 15% azelaic acid gel is [FDA-approved](https://drugcentral.org/label/66298d50-d4e2-4852-bbab-581389c488cb/view) for the bumps and pimples (papules and pustules) of mild to moderate rosacea — not for redness alone. For melasma, a [meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10339666/) has compared it with hydroquinone, a long-standing prescription option.

## Over-the-counter vs. prescription

Over-the-counter products typically contain lower concentrations than prescription gels and foams. If you have diagnosed rosacea or moderate to severe acne, a dermatologist can recommend the right strength — and rule out other causes.

## The bottom line

Azelaic acid earns its popularity: it has solid evidence for rosacea and good evidence for acne and pigmentation. Start slowly, protect your skin barrier, and use sunscreen. For persistent skin conditions, the most effective step is a visit to a board-certified dermatologist.
