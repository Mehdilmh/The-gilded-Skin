Choosing a fragrance in a store is hard enough. Online, you're choosing from a bottle photo and a few words. The good news is that those words — the notes, the fragrance family, the concentration — are more useful than they look, as long as you know what they can and can't tell you. This guide walks through each one, then covers the steps that save money: trying small first, checking the seller and storing your bottle well.

## Start from what you already like

The most reliable guide to a new fragrance is a fragrance you already wear, or one you've noticed on someone else and liked. Look up its published notes and fragrance family, then look for new options that share two or three of them. If you've never found one you love, think about scents you like outside of perfume — citrus peel, fresh laundry, vanilla, cut grass, a particular flower — and use those words as your starting point.

## How to read a note pyramid

Most fragrances are described in three layers:

- **Top notes** are what you notice first, in the opening minutes. Citrus, light fruits and fresh herbs often sit here.
- **Heart notes** (or middle notes) define the character once the opening fades — often florals.
- **Base notes** are the slower, deeper layer that lingers: woods, musk, vanilla, amber.

A pyramid is a description, not a recipe, and different retailers sometimes list the same fragrance with slightly different notes. Use it to judge style — fresh or warm, fruity or green, light or rich — rather than to predict exactly how it will smell on your skin.

## What EDT and EDP actually tell you

Labels such as eau de toilette (EDT) and eau de parfum (EDP) describe how concentrated a fragrance is, but they aren't standardized. Perfumers quoted by [Marie Claire](https://www.marieclaire.co.uk/beauty/fragrance/difference-between-eau-de-toilette-and-eau-de-parfum) note there's no official rule separating the two, and the ranges brands use overlap. Within one brand's line, an EDP is usually the more concentrated version — but the formula often differs too, so an EDT and EDP with the same name can smell noticeably different.

In practice: compare the specific fragrances, not just the label, and check which version a listing is for. Many fragrances are sold as an EDT, an EDP and sometimes an "intense" version with nearly identical bottles.

## Sensitive skin and fragrance allergy

Fragrance ingredients are one of the more common causes of allergic contact dermatitis; a [clinical review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14572300/) describes how it's diagnosed with patch testing. Labels don't make screening easy. FDA notes that in the U.S., fragrance ingredients may be [listed simply as "fragrance"](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics) on cosmetic labels, and ingredients that the EU requires brands to flag as [allergens](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/allergens-cosmetics) may not be named.

If your skin is sensitive, [test a new product on a small area](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/prevent-skin-problems/test-skin-care-products) before wider use, keep fragrance off broken or irritated skin, and consider spraying it on clothing instead. If you get a rash, stop using it and see a dermatologist if it doesn't settle.

## Check the listing and the seller

Fragrance is a category where the details in a listing matter:

- **Name and version.** Confirm the exact fragrance, concentration (EDT, EDP, parfum) and size in the title. Similar names in the same line are easy to confuse.
- **Seller.** Buying from a listing sold by the brand or the retailer itself lowers the risk of counterfeit or old stock.
- **On arrival.** Check that the box is sealed and the bottle, cap and labeling look right. If anything seems off, contact the seller and use the return process.

Be cautious with products sold as "inspired by" or "dupes" of famous perfumes, especially ones with look-alike names or packaging, and with listings that promise effects on attraction. Neither is something we can verify, and we don't recommend them.

## Store it well

FDA notes that [heat and sunlight](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/resources-consumers-cosmetics/cosmetics-safety-qa-shelf-life) can make cosmetics deteriorate before their expected shelf life. Keep bottles capped, out of direct sun and away from radiators and the shower. A drawer or closed cabinet is better than a sunny dresser, however pretty the display — our guide to [organizing a vanity](/how-to-organize-a-skincare-routine) covers storage zones.

## The bottom line

Start from fragrances you know you like, use the note pyramid to judge style, and treat EDT and EDP as rough labels rather than promises. Try a small size first, patch test if your skin is sensitive, and buy from a trustworthy seller.
