A lash curler is one of the smallest tools on a vanity and one of the most noticeable in the mirror: lifted lashes make eyes look more open, even without mascara. Heated versions have become popular because they promise a softer, longer-lasting curl without the pinch of a clamp. They also bring heat close to the most delicate skin on your face, so technique and hygiene matter.

## Heated vs. manual: how they work

A **manual curler** clamps lashes between a curved metal or plastic frame and a soft pad, bending them upward. It's quick and gives a defined curl, but squeezing too hard, tugging or using a worn pad can crimp or break lashes.

A **heated curler** uses gentle warmth to shape lashes, much like a miniature styling tool. Most are battery-powered wands or heated clamps with a comb or silicone pad. Instead of one firm squeeze, you hold or sweep the warmed surface against the lashes so they set in a lifted shape.

## Burn and eye safety

Heated curlers are designed for use near the eye, but they're still a heated tool. Conair's [instruction booklet](https://www.conair.com/on/demandware.static/-/Library-Sites-usConairShared/default/dw214f2502/Instructional%20Booklets/HEC3.pdf) for its heated lash curler, for example, warns against using a damaged unit, against using it while drowsy, and suggests checking with a doctor first if you have a skin condition or very sensitive skin. Your device's manual is the authority; read it before first use.

FDA's [eye cosmetic safety advice](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/eye-cosmetic-safety) is a useful baseline: wash your hands before applying eye products, and make sure anything you place near your eyes is clean.

## Hygiene: don't share, and keep it clean

FDA advises not sharing or swapping eye cosmetics, even with a close friend, and the same reasoning applies to curlers that touch your lashes. Conair's booklet likewise recommends that each person use their own unit. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's [eye makeup tips](https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/eye-makeup) focus on using products made for the eye area and keeping things clean to avoid irritation and infection.

- **After each use:** Wipe the pad or heated surface with a clean cloth once it has cooled, following any cleaning directions from the manufacturer.
- **Don't submerge** a battery-powered or rechargeable curler unless the manual says it's safe.
- **Store it dry and capped**, away from humid shower shelves. If your eye tools tend to get lost, see our guide to [organizing a makeup drawer](/how-to-organize-a-makeup-drawer).
- **Pause use** if you have an eye infection, and clean the tool before using it again.

## Replacing pads and parts

On a manual curler, the pad does a lot of work. Replace it as soon as it looks creased, cracked, hardened or worn down, because a worn pad lets metal meet lashes and can pinch or snap them. Many curlers come with spare pads; some manufacturers suggest regular replacement intervals, so check the packaging.

For heated curlers, check the comb or silicone surface for damage and replace the unit if it overheats, heats unevenly or the housing cracks.

## Technique for a soft, lasting curl

1. **Start clean.** Remove old mascara and make sure lashes are dry.
2. **Check the instructions on mascara order.** Some heated wands are designed to be used on bare lashes, others over set mascara. Manual curlers are usually used before mascara, since curling coated lashes can make them stiff and more prone to breaking.
3. **Let it heat, then test.** Touch the heated surface briefly to the back of your hand. It should feel warm, not hot.
4. **Work from the root out.** Place the curler at the base of the lashes without touching skin, hold or sweep upward gently, then move toward the tips in small steps.
5. **Finish and set.** Apply mascara if you haven't already. Our [day makeup look](/day-makeup-look) shows where lashes fit in a quick routine.

Lash extensions come with their own risks. The American Academy of Ophthalmology notes that [extension risks](https://www.aao.org/eye-health/drugs/eyelash-extension-facts-safety) include eyelid or corneal infection, allergic reactions to the glue and temporary or permanent lash loss, so adding heat and pressure on top isn't wise. If your eyes are generally reactive, our guide to [makeup for sensitive skin](/makeup-for-sensitive-skin) covers gentler eye-makeup habits. For persistent eye irritation, see an eye doctor.

## The bottom line

Heated and manual curlers both lift lashes; heated ones trade a quick clamp for gentle warmth. If you go heated, keep the warm part on your lashes and away from your lid, use the lowest effective setting and follow the manual. Whichever type you choose, keep it clean, replace worn pads, don't share it, and skip heat altogether if you wear extensions or have an eye condition.
