Ceramides show up on moisturizer labels more than almost any other "barrier" ingredient. Unlike many trend ingredients, they're something your skin already makes — which is part of why they've become a dermatologist favorite for dry, easily irritated skin. Here's what they are, what the evidence actually shows and how to choose a product without overthinking it.

## What ceramides are

Ceramides are lipids — fatty molecules — concentrated in the stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the skin. Together with other lipids, they fill the spaces between skin cells, a bit like mortar between bricks. That lipid layer is a large part of what people mean by the "skin barrier": it slows water loss from the skin and helps keep irritants out.

A 2025 review in *Experimental Dermatology* describes ceramides' roles in [creating a moisture barrier, preserving hydration and regulating inflammation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39912256/), and notes that changes in ceramide content are associated with skin conditions including atopic dermatitis (eczema). Ceramide levels and makeup also vary between people and over time.

## What happens when the barrier is struggling

When the barrier is disrupted — by harsh cleansing, cold dry air, over-exfoliation or a condition like eczema — skin tends to lose water more easily and react more readily to things it would normally tolerate. That can look like tightness, flaking, rough patches, stinging when you apply products, or redness. Moisturizers help by adding water-attracting ingredients, softening the surface and sealing moisture in. Ceramide moisturizers aim to go a step further by supplying some of the lipids the barrier is made of.

Our [cold-weather skin barrier routine](/cold-weather-skin-barrier-routine) covers the habits — shorter warm showers, gentle cleansers, a humidifier — that do a lot of the work alongside any moisturizer.

The research on ceramide moisturizers is encouraging but more modest than marketing suggests.

The 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in the *Indian Journal of Dermatology* found that [ceramide-containing moisturizers improved eczema severity scores more than other moisturizers](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37151263/), but the difference in transepidermal water loss — a measure of how much water escapes through the skin — wasn't significant. Only five studies met the criteria, so the authors' conclusions rest on a small evidence base. In practice, that suggests ceramides are a reasonable choice, not a magic ingredient.

A Cleveland Clinic dermatologist recommends [creams with ceramides for eczema](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/eczema-skin-care), noting that eczema-prone skin can have fewer ceramides. The AAD also publishes guidance on [choosing an eczema-friendly moisturizer](https://aad.org/public/diseases/eczema/childhood/itch-relief/select-moisturizer). If your eczema is flaring, spreading, cracking or interfering with sleep, see a board-certified dermatologist — moisturizer is part of care, not the whole plan.

## How to choose a ceramide moisturizer

The ingredient is only one part of the decision. Texture, fragrance and how much you'll actually use it matter just as much.

- **Pick the right texture.** The AAD notes that [ointments are the thickest and most effective for dry skin](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/pick-moisturizer), followed by creams; lotions are lighter. Many people use a cream on the face and something richer on very dry patches.
- **Go fragrance-free** if your skin is dry, sensitive or eczema-prone.
- **Look for supporting ingredients.** Ceramides are often paired with glycerin, hyaluronic acid, cholesterol, fatty acids, petrolatum, dimethicone or shea butter.
- **Choose something you'll finish.** A moisturizer only helps if you use it every day.

## Reading the label

Ceramides appear on ingredient lists under several names, which can be confusing. You'll commonly see **ceramide NP, ceramide AP and ceramide EOP**, and sometimes older names like ceramide 1, 3 or 6-II. Some formulas use **phytosphingosine**-based ceramides, which belong to the same family. Where an ingredient sits on the list gives a rough idea of how much is there, but ceramides are often used at low levels, so their position alone doesn't tell you how well a product works.

Be cautious about front-of-pack claims such as "repairs the barrier in 24 hours" — the label can't tell you that, and the research doesn't support sweeping promises.

## How to use it

The AAD recommends applying moisturizer [within a few minutes of washing](https://aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-basics/dry/dermatologists-tips-relieve-dry-skin), while skin is still slightly damp, so it traps that water in. In a typical routine, moisturizer goes after serums or treatments and before sunscreen in the morning. If you use retinoids or acids, a ceramide cream is a common way to keep skin comfortable; see our guide to [using retinol and vitamin C together](/retinol-and-vitamin-c-together) for layering basics. And if you'll be outdoors during the day, don't let a good moisturizer stand in for sunscreen — our guide to [reapplying sunscreen over makeup](/how-to-reapply-sunscreen-over-makeup) covers top-ups.

If dry skin doesn't improve after a couple of weeks of gentle care, the AAD suggests seeing a board-certified dermatologist.

## The bottom line

Ceramides are a natural part of your skin's barrier, and moisturizers that contain them are a sensible choice for dry, sensitized or eczema-prone skin. The evidence is promising but limited, so focus on the basics: a fragrance-free cream or ointment, applied to damp skin every day — and a dermatologist for anything that persists.
